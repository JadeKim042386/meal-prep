# Maillard and Caramelization

These are the two great browning reactions, and they are the single biggest source of "cooked" flavor in savory cooking. The Maillard reaction needs amino acids (protein) plus reducing sugars; caramelization needs only sugar. Both generate hundreds of new aroma compounds out of almost nothing — they are how you manufacture depth, roastiness, and color on demand.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [fact] Maillard = reducing sugar + amino acid → browning; meaningful onset ~140–165°C, peak development 165–200°C, char/bitter pyrolysis above ~230°C #maillard
- [fact] Caramelization = sugar decomposing alone, no protein needed; cross-verified onsets: fructose ~110°C, glucose ~150°C, sucrose ~160–170°C, maltose ~180°C #caramelization
- [ratio] Caramel deepens in stages: pale ~160–170°C → amber ~180°C → dark/bitter, then polymerizes ~180–200°C into the pigments caramelan/caramelen/caramelin #stages
- [fact] Maillard speeds up in alkaline conditions (a pinch of baking soda) and slows in acidic ones #pH
- [technique] Both reactions need a dry surface — water caps temperature at 100°C, far below either onset, so browning waits until moisture leaves #dry-surface
- [fact] Maillard yields savory/meaty/roasty notes (pyrazines, etc.); caramelization yields sweet/nutty/buttery then bitter notes — different flavor families #aroma

## Why It Works
Maillard is not one reaction but a cascade: a sugar's carbonyl group condenses with an amino group, rearranges (the Amadori rearrangement), then fragments and recombines into hundreds of compounds — nutty pyrazines, savory thiophenes, roasty furans. It runs fastest hot and dry, which is why a seared steak or 누룽지 (toasted rice crust) browns while a boiled one never does. Caramelization is simpler chemistry: pure sugar absorbing enough heat to break apart and recombine, with each sugar starting at its own temperature because their molecular bonds differ in strength. Crucially both stall under water, so the practical skill is the same — drive off surface moisture first, then push into the 140°C+ zone. Alkalinity strips protons that otherwise slow the Maillard cascade, so a whisper of baking soda visibly accelerates browning on onions or smashed potatoes.

## Creative Application
Browning is a flavor ingredient you can dial in. Brush mayonnaise (egg protein + oil) onto a sandwich exterior or fish before searing — the extra protein and fat supercharge Maillard for a deeper golden crust. Add a pinch of baking soda when caramelizing 양파 (onions) to cut a 45-minute job to 20 by nudging pH alkaline. For desserts, take sugar to amber (~180°C) before adding cream — you trade some sweetness for bitter-complex caramel that reads as "grown-up." Reverse the logic too: when you want a clean, bright dish (a fresh salad, a delicate steamed fish), deliberately avoid browning to keep flavors raw and high.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- No browning despite high heat → surface is wet or pan crowded; dry the food, reduce the batch, wait for the sizzle to subside.
- Bitter, acrid crust → pushed past ~200–230°C into pyrolysis; pull heat sooner or move food off the hottest zone.
- Caramel seizes/crystallizes → stirring or stray crystals; swirl don't stir, and brush down the pan sides with water.
- Caramel tastes flat-sweet, not complex → stopped too pale; take it to amber, accepting a touch of pleasant bitterness.
