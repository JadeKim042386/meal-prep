# Smoking

Smoking deposits the aroma compounds of smoldering wood onto food, simultaneously flavoring, coloring, and (historically) preserving it. The split between hot smoking (which cooks) and cold smoking (which only flavors/cures) is the first decision. Done well, smoke adds a deep, primal layer — guaiacol's "smoky taste" and syringol's "smoky aroma" — that no other technique can imitate.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [fact] Hot smoking ~45–90°C cooks and flavors at once; warm smoking ~25–45°C; cold smoking ~12–25°C flavors without cooking (so food must be cured first for safety) #temperature
- [fact] Smoke flavor comes mainly from lignin pyrolysis → phenols; ~60% of the phenolic fraction is guaiacol + syringol and their derivatives #chemistry
- [fact] Guaiacol (2-methoxyphenol) drives the smoky taste; syringol (2,6-dimethoxyphenol) drives the smoky aroma #key-compounds
- [technique] Wood's three components pyrolyze differently: cellulose → color-forming aldehydes, hemicellulose → furans, lignin → the all-important phenols #wood-breakdown
- [ratio] Best flavor comes from clean, low smoldering combustion ~300–400°C wood temp; hotter/oxygen-starved fires make acrid, sooty, PAH-heavy smoke #clean-smoke
- [fact] Smoke is ~380 compounds — phenols, aldehydes, ketones, organic acids, alcohols, esters; the acids also lower surface pH and aid preservation #composition

## Why It Works
When wood smolders, its polymers crack apart by heat (pyrolysis): lignin releases the methoxyphenols (guaiacol, syringol) that the nose and tongue read as "smoke," hemicellulose gives sweet-caramel furans, and cellulose gives color-building aldehydes. These compounds ride the smoke as droplets and vapor and condense onto the cool, slightly tacky surface of the food, so a slightly moist surface (a "pellicle") catches far more flavor than a dry one. Combustion temperature is everything: a clean ~300–400°C smolder produces the desirable aromatic phenols, while a too-hot or smothered fire produces creosote and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons that taste bitter and ashy. Cold smoke flavors without raising temperature enough to kill bacteria — which is exactly why cold-smoked items must be salted/cured first.

## Creative Application
Think of smoke as a seasoning you apply in grams, not as a cooking method. Cold-smoke salt, butter, or 고춧가루 (gochugaru) for an hour and you get a smoke-flavored pantry ingredient to deploy anywhere — no smoker needed at serving time. Match wood to food by phenol intensity: mild alder/fruitwoods for fish and pork, assertive hickory/oak for beef. Or build a hybrid: smoke a pork belly low to deposit phenols, then braise it (moist heat) so the smoke flavor permeates the rendered, gelatin-rich result — a smoked-then-braised 삼겹살 hits both crust aroma and silky texture.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Bitter, acrid, ashy flavor → fire too hot or oxygen-starved making creosote; run a clean, ventilated smolder and avoid thick white smoke (aim for thin blue).
- Smoke won't stick → surface too dry; let a tacky pellicle form (air-dry in fridge) before smoking.
- Cold-smoked food spoils → it was never cooked; cure/salt thoroughly first and keep cold-smoking in the safe 12–25°C band.
- Flavor too faint or too harsh → wrong wood for the protein; scale phenol intensity to the food's delicacy.
