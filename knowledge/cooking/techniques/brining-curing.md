# Brining and Curing

Brining and curing both use salt to change protein — for juiciness, seasoning, texture, and (in curing) preservation and color. Brining seasons and keeps meat moist; curing goes further, using salt plus nitrite/nitrate over time to make charcuterie like bacon, ham, and 장조림-style preserved foods. Salt is the lever; understanding osmosis and the right percentages turns guesswork into control.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [ratio] Wet brine: dissolve salt to ~1% of total weight (meat + water) for through-seasoning; classic strong brines run higher and shorter #wet-brine
- [ratio] Dry brine: salt at ~0.75–1.5% of meat weight (≈1.5% is a reliable poultry target), applied 12–48 h ahead, no water #dry-brine
- [fact] Salt dissolves muscle proteins (myosin) so they hold more water and bind it during cooking — the meat cooks up juicier and seasons throughout #osmosis
- [fact] Osmosis/diffusion drives it: salt moves into the meat and water redistributes; dry brine reabsorbs its own drawn-out moisture as a concentrated brine #diffusion
- [fact] Cure #1 (Prague Powder #1) = 6.25% sodium nitrite + 93.75% salt, dyed pink; used ~0.25% of meat weight (≈1.1 g/kg) for short cures #cure1
- [fact] Nitrite fixes the pink cured color, blocks Clostridium botulinum, and gives the characteristic "cured" ham/bacon flavor #nitrite
- [technique] Equilibrium brining (% salt of total weight, can't over-salt) vs. time-based strong brining (faster but must pull on time) #methods

## Why It Works
Salt does three things to protein. First, it seasons by diffusion — sodium and chloride migrate into the muscle down their concentration gradient. Second, and more importantly, dissolved salt partially unravels and solubilizes the muscle protein myosin so the meat's fibers swell and hold onto water rather than squeezing it out when heated — that is why a brined chicken breast stays juicy past the point a plain one would dry. Dry brining works the same way: salt first pulls surface moisture out by osmosis, that moisture dissolves the salt into a tiny concentrated brine, and the whole thing is then reabsorbed, seasoning deeply with no added water to dilute flavor. Curing adds nitrite, which reacts with myoglobin to lock in pink color, inhibits botulism in the low-oxygen interior of sausages, and contributes the unmistakable cured tang — which is exactly why the 0.25% dose is non-negotiable for safety.

## Creative Application
Make dry-brining your default for any roast or 닭가슴살 meal prep: salt at ~1% the night before, leave uncovered in the fridge, and you get seasoned-through, juicy meat plus a dry surface that browns hard — two wins from one step. For a flavored brine, dissolve salt to 1% of total weight plus aromatics (간장, garlic, ginger, star anise) and the seasoning rides in with the salt for an infused result. To explore curing safely, do a short equilibrium cure with Cure #1 at 0.25% to make home bacon — precise percentages mean you genuinely cannot over-cure.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Meat too salty → strong time-based brine left too long; switch to equilibrium method (% of total weight) so it self-limits.
- Watery, bland result → under-salted or too short; hit ~1% and give dry brine 12–48 h to reabsorb.
- Spongy, ham-like texture in a plain brine → over-brined; shorten time or lower salt %.
- Cured product unsafe / gray → wrong nitrite dose; use exactly 0.25% Cure #1 by meat weight and never freehand nitrite.
