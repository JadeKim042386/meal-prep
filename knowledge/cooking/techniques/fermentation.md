# Fermentation

Fermentation enlists microbes — bacteria, yeasts, molds — to transform food, generating acids, alcohol, CO₂, and a flood of umami and aroma compounds while preserving it. The four workhorses for cooks are lacto-fermentation (김치, sauerkraut), yeast (bread, alcohol), koji/mold (된장, 간장, miso), and acetic/vinegar fermentation. It is the deepest flavor-creation tool in the kitchen: it makes compounds that simply do not exist in the raw ingredient.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [ratio] Lacto-fermenting vegetables: 2–3% salt by weight is the sweet spot (firm veg ~2%, high-moisture veg like cucumber 3–3.5%); LAB tolerate ~1.5–5% #salinity
- [fact] Salt selectively favors salt-tolerant lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus) while suppressing spoilage organisms, then they acidify the brine and self-preserve #lacto
- [fact] Lacto-fermentation is anaerobic — keep vegetables submerged under brine; LAB convert sugars → lactic acid, dropping pH and souring #anaerobic
- [technique] Yeast (Saccharomyces) ferments sugars → ethanol + CO₂; CO₂ leavens bread, ethanol is the substrate for vinegar #yeast
- [fact] Koji mold (Aspergillus oryzae) secretes proteases and amylases that pre-digest protein into umami amino acids and starch into sweet sugars #koji
- [fact] Acetic fermentation: acetic acid bacteria (Acetobacter) oxidize ethanol → acetic acid using oxygen — this is aerobic, the opposite of lacto #vinegar
- [fact] Soy sauce/miso: A. oryzae digests soybeans+wheat into koji, then ~18% salt brine ferments for months; salt-tolerant proteases work even in high sodium #jang

## Why It Works
Each pathway is a different microbe doing chemistry you cannot do with heat. Lacto-fermentation is a controlled competition: salt at 2–3% handicaps spoilage microbes just enough that naturally-present Lactobacillus wins, eating sugars and excreting lactic acid that pushes pH below the danger zone — sour, crunchy, and shelf-stable. Koji is enzymatic alchemy: the mold's proteases cleave proteins into free glutamate (pure umami) and its amylases cleave starch into sweetness, which is why 된장 and 간장 taste so savory-deep — they are essentially predigested. Yeast splits sugar into ethanol and CO₂; that ethanol is then food for Acetobacter, which oxidizes it with oxygen into acetic acid, so wine left open becomes vinegar via the "mother" biofilm. The throughline is that microbes manufacture acids and amino acids that read as brightness and depth.

## Creative Application
Use ferments as concentrated flavor bombs, not just side dishes. Stir a spoon of miso or 된장 into a pan sauce or butter and you inject instant koji-made umami plus salt — a "umami paste" that deepens anything from soup to roasted vegetables. Make a quick lacto-ferment of a vegetable at 2.5% brine for 3–5 days to get a sour, fizzy, alive condiment that brightens rich dishes (a flavor-bridge between fatty meat and acid). Or build your own koji-cured protein: rub miso or shio-koji onto chicken or fish overnight — the proteases tenderize and pre-season, giving roast-deep flavor with no extra browning.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Mushy/spoiled lacto-ferment → salinity too low or veg exposed to air; hit 2–3% salt and keep everything submerged under brine.
- Kahm yeast / mold on top → surface contact with air; use a weight, top up brine, skim white film promptly.
- Vinegar won't form → no oxygen or no live Acetobacter; give it airflow (cloth cover) and a vinegar mother to seed.
- Koji ferment too salty/flat → salt level wrong for the long ferment; jang-style needs high salt (~18%) but shorter shio-koji marinades need far less.
