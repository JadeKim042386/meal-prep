# Emulsification

Emulsification is the art of forcing two ingredients that hate each other — oil and water — to live together as a single creamy phase. Master it and you unlock mayo, aioli, hollandaise, vinaigrette, beurre blanc, and an infinite family of dressings and pan sauces. It is the single highest-leverage skill for turning thin liquids into luxurious, clinging, glossy sauces.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [fact] An emulsion is microscopic droplets of one liquid (the dispersed phase) suspended in another (the continuous phase). Oil-in-water = mayo, vinaigrette, hollandaise, beurre blanc; water-in-oil = butter #emulsion
- [fact] Droplets naturally want to coalesce; an EMULSIFIER coats them and keeps them apart electrostatically or by forming a physical film #emulsifier
- [fact] Egg yolk emulsifies via lecithin (a phospholipid) plus yolk proteins; one yolk can emulsify ~200 mL+ of oil into mayo #lecithin
- [fact] Mustard contains mucilage that both emulsifies and stabilizes — a key reason vinaigrettes hold #mustard
- [ratio] Vinaigrette: 3 parts oil : 1 part acid by volume (classic); dial to 2:1 for brighter, 4:1 for mellower #vinaigrette
- [ratio] Mayonnaise: ~1 egg yolk + 1 cup (≈240 mL) oil + ¼ cup acid; yolk:oil works because lecithin is abundant #mayo
- [ratio] Beurre blanc: an acid/shallot reduction enriched with cold butter; butter is ~80% fat, 16% water — the milk solids/proteins emulsify the fat into the water #beurre-blanc
- [fact] Beurre blanc and hollandaise are TEMPERATURE-FRAGILE: above ~58 °C (136 °F) butterfat membranes break and oil leaks out; serve and hold gently warm, never near boiling #temperature
- [technique] Build emulsions by adding the dispersed phase SLOWLY at first (drops, then thin stream) while agitating hard — high shear shrinks droplets and the emulsifier coats them before they merge #shear

## Why It Works
Oil and water do not mix because water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other and exclude nonpolar oil. An emulsifier is amphiphilic: one end is hydrophilic (water-loving), the other lipophilic (oil-loving). It parks at the oil-water interface, lowering interfacial tension and wrapping each oil droplet in a coat that repels its neighbors. Vigorous whisking or blending supplies the mechanical energy (shear) to rip the oil into tiny droplets — and smaller droplets pack more tightly, which is exactly why a properly made mayo is thick and stiff even though it is mostly oil. Stability also depends on the ratio of phases: too much oil relative to water and emulsifier crowds the droplets until they touch and merge (the emulsion "breaks").

## Creative Application
Think of emulsification as a delivery system for flavor: any fat can carry fat-soluble aromatics (chili oil, brown butter, herb oil, sesame) and any water phase can carry acid, umami, and water-soluble aromatics (miso, fish sauce, citrus, gochujang). Example 1: a 간장-마늘 vinaigrette using toasted sesame oil + neutral oil as the fat and 간장 + rice vinegar + a dab of mustard as the acid/emulsifier — drizzle over grilled vegetables. Example 2: a "broken" pan sauce on purpose — let butter and reduction split into a glossy beurre noisette drizzle for nutty depth, then re-emulsify a portion for a two-texture plate. Use soy lecithin (0.3–0.6%) or a pinch of xanthan to stabilize otherwise impossible emulsions like an airy citrus foam or a shelf-stable herb dressing.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Broken emulsion (oil pooled out): start fresh with a teaspoon of water (or a new yolk for mayo) in a clean bowl and whisk the broken sauce back in DROP BY DROP — you are rebuilding the continuous phase. #fix
- Hollandaise/beurre blanc split from overheating: pull from heat, cool to ~43 °C (110 °F), whisk in a splash of cold water or an ice cube to re-form. #fix
- Adding oil too fast at the start = the most common failure; go drop-by-drop until the emulsion "takes," then speed up. #fix
- Too thick? Loosen with a few drops of water, not more oil. Cream adds proteins/phospholipids that stabilize fragile emulsions. #fix
