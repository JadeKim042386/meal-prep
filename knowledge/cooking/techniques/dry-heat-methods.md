# Dry Heat Methods

Dry heat is any method that cooks without water as the carrier: air (roasting, baking), radiant heat (grilling, broiling), or hot fat (sautéing, pan-frying, deep-frying, searing). Because there is no water cap at 100°C, surface temperatures climb past the browning threshold, unlocking the Maillard reaction and caramelization that build a roasted, savory crust. Mastering dry heat is mostly about managing the gradient between a screaming-hot surface and a gently-cooking interior.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [fact] Water boils at 100°C, so anything wet stays ≤100°C — too cool to brown; dry heat lets surfaces reach 140–200°C where browning runs #browning
- [fact] Maillard browning becomes meaningful ~140–165°C and accelerates toward 165–200°C; above ~230°C (450°F) it tips into bitter pyrolysis/char #maillard
- [technique] Searing/roasting/grilling are radiant or conductive air-fat methods; deep-frying uses oil as a near-instant heat-transfer fluid #heat-transfer
- [ratio] Deep-frying runs 175–190°C (350–375°F): hot enough to flash surface water into a steam barrier that keeps oil out and crisps the crust #deep-fry
- [fact] Oil choice is bounded by smoke point — refined avocado ~250–270°C, ghee ~250°C, canola ~205–245°C, grapeseed ~215°C, butter ~150–177°C, EVOO ~163–210°C #smoke-point
- [technique] Sauté = high heat, thin layer, constant motion in a little fat; pan-fry = medium heat, more fat, less motion for a set crust #stovetop

## Why It Works
Heat moves into food three ways, and dry methods exploit all of them: conduction (pan to steak), convection (hot air swirling in an oven), and radiation (broiler coil, glowing coals). Because the surface is not capped at 100°C, it dehydrates and climbs into the Maillard window, where amino acids and reducing sugars condense into hundreds of new roasted, nutty, meaty aroma compounds. In deep-frying the oil acts as a uniform 175–190°C bath; the food's own moisture vaporizes outward, producing the bubbling that both drives a crisp crust and blocks oil absorption. The cook's real job is managing two clocks at once — a fast surface clock (browning) and a slow interior clock (doneness) — by trading heat intensity against time and distance from the source.

## Creative Application
Treat the crust as a flavor layer you build deliberately. Reverse-sear: bring a thick 삼겹살 or steak gently to near-target internal temp in a low oven, then blast the surface in a ripping-hot pan for 60–90 seconds — even color, edge-to-edge pink interior, maximum crust. Or "double-dry": pat protein bone-dry and salt it hours ahead so the surface is desiccated and browns the instant it hits metal. For vegetables, high-heat roasting (220°C+) caramelizes natural sugars in broccoli or carrots into something candy-savory you cannot get from steaming.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Pan steams instead of sears → surface is wet or pan is crowded/cool; dry food, work in batches, preheat until fat shimmers.
- Burnt outside, raw inside → heat too high for thickness; lower heat or finish in the oven (reverse-sear logic).
- Greasy fried food → oil too cool (under 165°C) so no steam barrier forms; recover temperature between batches.
- Smoke and acrid flavor → oil above its smoke point; switch to a higher-smoke-point refined oil.
