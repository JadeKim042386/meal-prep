# Ratios and Formulas

A ratio is a recipe freed from specific quantities: once you know that vinaigrette is 3:1 oil to acid, you can make a teaspoon or a gallon, with any oil and any acid, no recipe required. Memorizing a handful of core ratios converts cooking from following instructions into composing — you scale, substitute, and invent within proven proportions. (Many ratios are most reliable *by weight*; volume versions are approximations.)

## Principle / Mechanism
- [ratio] Vinaigrette — **3:1 oil:acid** (loosen toward 2:1 for more bite) #dressing
- [ratio] Wet brine — **~5% salt by weight of water** (≈20:1 water:salt; ~50 g salt/L) #brine
- [ratio] Dry brine / general seasoning — **~0.75–1% salt by weight of food** #salt
- [ratio] Pasta water — **~1% salt** (≈1 Tbsp table salt per ~4 L) #pasta
- [ratio] Roux — **1:1 fat:flour by weight**; ~1 oz (≈2 Tbsp) each thickens 1 cup liquid for a medium sauce #thickener
- [ratio] Custard (free-standing, baked) — **~1 large egg : 120 ml (½ cup) dairy**; loosely "2 parts liquid : 1 part egg" #custard
- [ratio] Crème anglaise / pourable custard — **~4 parts milk-cream : 1 part yolk : 1 part sugar** #custard
- [ratio] Pie/short dough (3-2-1) — **3 flour : 2 fat : 1 water by weight** #dough
- [ratio] Mayonnaise — **~1 egg yolk : 1 cup (≈240 ml) oil** (yolk lecithin emulsifies up to ~this much) #emulsion
- [ratio] Hollandaise — **~1 yolk : 60–85 g butter** (book ratio ~6 yolks : 1 lb butter) #sauce
- [ratio] Simple syrup — **1:1 sugar:water** (rich syrup 2:1) #syrup
- [ratio] Rice pilaf / absorption — **~2 parts liquid : 1 part rice** (variety-dependent) #rice
- [ratio] White stock — **~3 parts water : 2 parts bones**; mirepoix ~2 onion:1 carrot:1 celery #stock
- [ratio] Baker's percentage — flour = **100%**; all else as % of flour (e.g. ~60–70% water, ~2% salt, ~1% yeast) #bread

## Why It Works
Ratios encode the physics and chemistry that make a preparation succeed, independent of batch size. Bread is a system of flour, hydration, salt, and leavening in balance, so expressing each as a percentage of flour keeps gluten development, fermentation rate, and seasoning constant whether you bake one loaf or fifty. An emulsion holds only while there is enough emulsifier (lecithin in egg yolk) to coat the dispersed oil droplets, so the yolk:oil ratio is really a stability limit. A roux thickens because starch granules in the flour gelatinize and trap liquid; 1:1 fat:flour ensures every starch granule is coated to prevent clumping. Because these proportions reflect mechanisms rather than tastes, they hold across ingredients — which is exactly what makes them creative tools.

## Creative Application
Lock the ratio, swap the ingredients: a 3:1 vinaigrette becomes endless dressings by changing the oil (sesame, walnut), the acid (rice vinegar, yuzu, lime), and add-ins (된장, honey, mustard as emulsifier). Use baker's percentage to design a new bread or batter by deciding hydration and salt as % of flour. Scale any formula by setting the weight of the "100%" ingredient. Example: to invent a Korean-leaning dressing, hold 3:1 and use 들기름(perilla oil) : rice vinegar, plus a spoon of 간장 and honey — proportions guaranteed, flavor free to roam.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Measuring ratios by volume when weight is required (flour, salt across brands) drifts badly — weigh
- Adding oil too fast in mayo/vinaigrette breaks the emulsion — stream slowly; rescue a broken emulsion by whisking it into a fresh yolk or a teaspoon of mustard/water
- Treating ratios as rigid: they're starting points — adjust hydration, acid, or fat to taste, then note the new ratio
- Over-roux'd or over-reduced sauces turn pasty — thin with more liquid; under-thick → cook a little more or add a slurry
