# Balancing Flavors

Balancing is the act of tuning the five basic tastes — salty, sweet, sour, bitter, umami — plus richness and heat so no single sensation dominates and the dish tastes "complete." Because tastes interact (some suppress, some amplify each other), balancing is rarely about adding the obvious missing thing; it's about the counter-move. Mastering these interactions lets you fix any dish and design new flavor profiles on purpose.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [principle] The basic tastes are salty, sweet, sour, bitter, umami; balance is achieved by their ratios, not by any one alone #taste
- [fact] Salt suppresses bitterness and amplifies sweetness and umami #suppression
- [fact] Sweetness suppresses sourness and bitterness; acid cuts richness/sweetness and lifts a heavy dish #contrast
- [fact] Umami deepens savoriness and can make a dish taste "fuller" so it needs less salt #umami
- [fact] Fat rounds and carries flavor, tames heat (capsaicin is fat-soluble) and softens sharp edges #fat
- [principle] Fix by counterbalance: too salty → add acid/sweet/fat or bulk; too sweet → acid/salt/bitter; too sour → sweet/fat/salt; too bitter → salt/sweet/fat; too flat → salt then acid then umami #fix

## Why It Works
Taste perception is not a set of independent meters — receptors and the brain integrate the signals, so one taste changes how strongly we register another. Sodium ions physically block bitterness transduction and free sweet/umami signals to register more strongly, which is why salt "balances." Sourness and sweetness are mutually masking, so a too-sour sauce reads balanced once sugar is added even though the acid is still there. Fat doesn't change the taste molecules but coats the tongue, slowing and smoothing delivery and dissolving fat-soluble irritants like capsaicin, which is why dairy calms chili heat. Because these are perceptual interactions, small counter-additions can rebalance a dish far more efficiently than removing what's "too much."

## Creative Application
Build a target profile by choosing two or three tastes to feature and one to use as a quiet counterweight — e.g. Thai 똠얌/똠카 balances salty (fish sauce) + sour (lime) + sweet (palm sugar) + heat, with each tasted in at the end. To design a dish, start with salt + umami as the savory base, add acid for lift, then a touch of sweet to round, and bitterness (char, greens, coffee) for sophistication. Example fix: an over-rich coconut curry tastes "heavy and dull" → a squeeze of lime (acid) and a pinch of salt cuts and lifts it without diluting.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Chasing one taste in a loop (more salt → more sour → more sweet…): change only one variable, taste, wait, then reassess
- Adding acid/salt while still hot can mislead — flavors read differently at serving temperature; taste near final temp
- "Bland" almost always means under-salted or under-acidified, not under-spiced
- Over-corrected (over-sweet/sour): the only real fix is to dilute or scale up the batch
