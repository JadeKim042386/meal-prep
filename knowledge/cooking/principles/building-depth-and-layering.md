# Building Depth and Layering

Depth is the difference between a dish that tastes thin and one-dimensional and one that tastes "developed" — savory, complex, and lingering. It is built deliberately by *layering*: seasoning and developing flavor at every stage, stacking umami sources, and capturing browned compounds rather than relying on a single late addition. Understanding the levers lets you make even simple ingredients taste like they cooked for hours.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [principle] Build flavor in layers: season and develop at each stage (sweat aromatics, brown proteins, deglaze, simmer, finish) rather than dumping everything at once #layering
- [fact] Maillard browning and caramelization create hundreds of new savory/roasty/sweet aroma compounds absent in raw ingredients #browning
- [ratio] Umami synergy: glutamate + a nucleotide (inosinate IMP or guanylate GMP) is ~8× more intense than glutamate alone — combine, don't just stack one type #umami
- [pairing] Classic synergy pairs: 다시마(kombu, glutamate) + 가쓰오부시(bonito, inosinate); tomato + anchovy; mushroom (GMP) + meat (IMP) #synergy
- [technique] Fond (the browned residue stuck to the pan) is concentrated flavor; deglaze with liquid to dissolve it back into the dish #fond
- [technique] Reduction concentrates dissolved flavor and body by evaporating water #reduction

## Why It Works
Depth is chemical density: more distinct flavor and aroma molecules, present in complementary proportions. Browning reactions (Maillard between amino acids and sugars; caramelization of sugars) generate large families of new volatile compounds — roasty, nutty, meaty, malty — that simply don't exist in the raw food, which is why a seared, deglazed base tastes deeper than a boiled one. Umami synergy is a receptor effect: glutamate binds the T1R1/T1R3 umami receptor, and bound nucleotides (IMP/GMP) lock the receptor in a more active conformation, multiplying the signal up to roughly eightfold. Layering matters because flavors built and seasoned at each step (and the fond captured along the way) accumulate, whereas everything added at the end stays surface-level and disjointed.

## Creative Application
Treat a dish as a stack: (1) brown aromatics/proteins for Maillard depth, (2) deglaze to recover the fond, (3) introduce a complementary umami pair early for extraction, (4) simmer/reduce to concentrate, (5) brighten and salt at the end. To deepen any soup or stew cheaply, add a glutamate source (다시마, tomato paste, parmesan rind, 된장) *and* a nucleotide source (dried mushroom, anchovy, 가쓰오부시) so synergy kicks in. Example: a quick 된장찌개 gains long-cooked depth by browning the aromatics first and pairing 된장 (glutamate) with a few dried anchovies and a piece of 다시마 (inosinate/glutamate stock) — eightfold savoriness from pantry items.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- "Thin/flat" broth: usually missing browning, umami synergy, or salt — sear a base, add a glutamate+nucleotide pair, then season
- Wasting the fond by boiling it off or discarding it — deglaze and reincorporate
- Crowding the pan steams instead of browns, so no Maillard depth develops — high heat, dry surfaces, batches
- Bitter/acrid depth: pushed browning into burning (pyrolysis) — pull back before black; scorched fond can't be saved
- Stacking only one umami type tastes monotone — combine glutamate with a nucleotide for true synergy
