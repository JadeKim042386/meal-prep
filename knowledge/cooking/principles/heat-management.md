# Heat Management

Heat is the element that physically transforms food, and *managing* it — choosing the transfer mode, the intensity, and when to stop — is what separates browned-and-juicy from gray-and-dry. The core skills are matching heat level to the goal (browning vs. gentle cooking), reading internal temperature, and accounting for carryover. Control heat and you control texture, doneness, and browning all at once.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [fact] Heat moves by **conduction** (direct contact, e.g. pan/griddle), **convection** (fluid/air, e.g. boiling, oven, frying), and **radiation** (infrared, e.g. broiler/grill/embers) #transfer
- [fact] **Maillard browning** runs fast at ~**140–165°C (285–330°F)**; surfaces must be dry and above 100°C since water-logged food stalls at boiling #browning
- [fact] **Caramelization** (sugar pyrolysis) needs higher temps, generally ~**160–180°C+ (320–360°F+)**; beyond that, acrid pyrolysis/burning #caramel
- [fact] **Carryover cooking**: internal temp keeps rising ~**5–10°F (≈3–6°C)** after removal (more for large roasts), as heat migrates from hot exterior to cooler center #carryover
- [technique] Pull meat ~5–10°F **below** target and rest 10–15 min so it finishes to temp and juices redistribute #resting
- [principle] High heat for surface transformation (sear, crisp, char); low/gentle heat for even interior cooking and delicate proteins #intensity
- [fact] Water caps temperature near 100°C, so moist methods are gentle and even but cannot brown; dry methods reach browning temps #moist-vs-dry

## Why It Works
Heat is energy that drives transformation, and the *rate* and *mode* of delivery determine the outcome. Conduction and radiation can push a surface well past water's boiling point, enabling Maillard and caramelization, while convection through water or steam is clamped near 100°C and so cooks gently and evenly without browning. Carryover happens because cooking creates a temperature gradient — the exterior is much hotter than the measured center — and after you remove the food, that stored heat keeps flowing inward until equilibrium, so the center climbs several degrees on its own. Resting also lets muscle fibers relax and reabsorb juices that high heat had squeezed out. Knowing these mechanisms means you stop cooking *before* the target and choose the transfer mode that matches whether you want a crust or a tender interior.

## Creative Application
Decide the goal first, then the mode: want a crust? Use ripping-hot conduction (cast iron) or radiation (broiler), dry the surface, don't crowd. Want a silky, evenly-cooked interior (custard, fish, eggs)? Use gentle convection (low oven, water bath, bain-marie) so the outside doesn't overcook before the center sets. Combine modes for the best of both — sear then finish in a low oven (reverse-sear logic), or braise low and slow (collagen → gelatin) then blast high heat at the end for color. Example: a thick steak gets reverse-seared — gentle oven to ~5–10°F below target, rest, then a fierce conduction sear for crust without overcooking the middle.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Overcrowding the pan drops its temperature and traps steam → no browning; cook in batches, preheat well, dry surfaces
- Ignoring carryover → overcooked center; pull early and rest, measure internal temp rather than time
- Trying to brown wet food (just-washed, frozen, watery) → it stalls and steams; pat dry first
- Too-high heat on sugars/dairy/aromatics → bitter burnt notes (pyrolysis, scorched milk solids); lower heat or stir/move constantly
- Cutting meat immediately → juices run out; rest 10–15 min so they redistribute
