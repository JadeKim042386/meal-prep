# Seasoning and Salting

Salting is the single highest-leverage skill in cooking: *how much*, *when*, and *what kind* of salt you use determines whether ingredients taste fully like themselves. The goal is not "salty" food — it is food whose own flavors are amplified and whose bitterness is muted. Salting by weight (a percentage of the food) makes seasoning repeatable and frees you from recipes.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [ratio] Season proteins and many dishes at ~0.75–1% salt by total weight for a reliable, well-seasoned (not salty) baseline #salt
- [ratio] Dry brine: ~1% salt by weight of the meat (some go to ~1.5–2% for cure-like effect), rest 8–24h uncovered in the fridge #brine
- [ratio] Wet brine: ~5% salt by weight of water (≈20:1 water:salt), e.g. 50 g salt per 1 L water #brine
- [ratio] Pasta water: ~1% salt (roughly 1 Tbsp table salt per 4 qt / ~4 L) so noodles season from the inside #pasta
- [fact] Salt enhances aroma release: added during cooking it drives some aromas off; added at the very end it releases them as you eat #aroma
- [fact] Salt suppresses bitterness and boosts perceived sweetness and savoriness, so it "balances" as well as enhances #perception
- [fact] Salt by weight ≠ by volume: kosher/coarse salts are far less dense than table salt, so equal spoons differ greatly — weigh, or recalibrate per brand #measurement

## Why It Works
Sodium ions activate salt receptors but also reshape the whole flavor picture: at the periphery and in the brain, salt suppresses bitterness and lets sweet and umami signals dominate, which is why a pinch of salt makes melon sweeter and coffee less bitter. Salt is hygroscopic and ionic, so given time it dissolves on the surface, draws out moisture, then reabsorbs as a concentrated brine that diffuses inward — this is why early salting (dry brining) seasons throughout and improves texture (partial protein dissolution holds water, yielding juicier, more tender meat), whereas last-second salt sits only on the surface for a sharper "pop." Salting at every stage builds seasoning in layers so the final dish needs no heavy correction.

## Creative Application
Salt early for penetration and tenderness (meats, beans, braises, dough resting), salt mid-cooking to season aromatics ("salt the onions so they taste like onions"), and salt at the very end (flaky finishing salt) for contrast and aroma pop. To make a dish you've never cooked, weigh the main ingredients and target ~1% salt total, then taste and nudge. Example: 불고기 marinade seasons from within; a final pinch of flaky salt on the grilled slices adds bursts of contrast. For soups/sauces destined for reduction, under-salt early — concentration raises salinity.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Salting only at the end = surface-salty but bland inside; season in layers instead
- Switching salt brands without recalibrating volume = wildly over/under-salted; weigh salt or learn your brand's spoon
- Over-salted: dilute with unsalted stock/water, add bulk (potato/rice/beans), more acid + fat to mask, or stretch into a larger batch
- Salting steaks 10–40 min before cooking (the "wet surface" window) gives a worse sear — either salt right before, or dry-brine ≥45 min so the surface re-dries
