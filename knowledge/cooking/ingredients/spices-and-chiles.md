# Spices and Chiles
Spices are dried seeds, barks, roots, and fruits whose flavor is locked in fat-soluble volatile oils — which means how you wake them up (heat + fat) matters as much as which ones you choose. Chiles are a special case: they add aroma AND a non-taste pungency from capsaicin that you measure, not season, by feel. Mastering blooming, toasting, and whole-vs-ground control turns a spice rack from a set of dusty jars into a deliberate flavor synthesizer.

## Behavior / Role
- [technique] Toasting = whole spices in a DRY hot pan; it volatilizes and lightly browns the oils (Maillard on the spice's own sugars/proteins), deepening aroma — toast whole, not ground, since ground burns in seconds #toasting
- [technique] Blooming = ground spices in HOT FAT; fat dissolves the fat-soluble aromatics and distributes them through the dish (the foundation of curries, chili, and tarka/tadka) #blooming
- [fact] Whole spices keep their oils sealed and stay potent for ~1–2 years; ground spices oxidize and fade within months — grind just before use for max impact #whole-vs-ground
- [fact] Capsaicin is the pungent compound in chiles; it binds the TRPV1 heat/pain receptor (it is a chemical irritant, not a taste) and is fat- and alcohol-soluble but not water-soluble — dairy fat (casein) cuts it, water spreads it #capsaicin
- [fact] Scoville Heat Units (SHU) measure capsaicin concentration by dilution: jalapeño 2,500–8,000; serrano 8,000–20,000; cayenne 30,000–50,000; habanero 100,000–350,000; ghost (bhut jolokia) ~850,000–1,040,000 #scoville
- [fact] A 350,000-SHU habanero is ~100× hotter than a 3,500-SHU jalapeño; heat scales geometrically, so dose conservatively #scoville
- [fact] Sichuan pepper isn't on the Scoville scale — its tingle comes from hydroxy-alpha-sanshool acting on touch nerves, not capsaicin #numbing
- [pairing] Regional blends encode whole cuisines: garam masala (warm sweet spices, India), berbere (chile + warm spices, Ethiopia), ras el hanout (N. Africa), five-spice (China), za'atar (thyme+sumac+sesame, Levant), shichimi togarashi (Japan) #blends

## Selection / Handling
- [fact] Buy whole where possible and small quantities often; aroma when you crush a pinch is the freshness test #selection
- [technique] Toast over medium heat just until fragrant (30 sec–a few minutes; chiles 2–6 min), tossing constantly — pull at the first whiff, scorched spice is acrid and unfixable #technique
- [fact] Dried chiles vary by role: anchos/mulatos for sweet body, guajillo/New Mexico for color and mild fruit, chipotle for smoke, árbol/cayenne for clean heat; toast and rehydrate for sauces #dried-chiles
- [fact] Most chile heat lives in the white pith (placenta) holding the seeds, not the flesh — scrape pith to dial heat down while keeping flavor #handling
- [fact] Store away from heat, light, and air; whole ~2 yr, ground ~6 mo as a practical potency window #storage

## Creative Application & Substitution
Build a dish's spine by blooming a fat base (onion/garlic/ginger + ground spices) then layering toasted-and-ground whole spices for top-note complexity — this is the curry/chili/mole architecture. Separate the two jobs chiles do: use a mild, fruity dried chile (ancho, guajillo) for *flavor and color*, and add a small dose of a hot one (árbol, cayenne, or fresh) for *heat* — that's how you get deep flavor without melting faces. To substitute heat by role, match SHU not name: ~3 serranos ≈ 1 jalapeño's flavor at higher heat; a pinch of cayenne or gochugaru can stand in for fresh chile. For a missing blend, reverse-engineer it from its parts (garam masala ≈ cumin+coriander+cardamom+cinnamon+clove+pepper).

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Spice tastes bitter/burnt → toasted too hot/long; start over, pull at first aroma.
- Dish reads flat despite lots of spice → spices were old/ground, or never bloomed in fat; bloom fresh.
- Too spicy → add fat/dairy (yogurt, coconut milk, butter), acid, or sugar; bulk up with more base ingredients; water makes it worse.
- Raw, dusty curry flavor → you didn't cook the ground spices in fat long enough; bloom until the oil separates and smells nutty.
