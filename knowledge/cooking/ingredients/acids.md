# Acids
Acid is the most underused seasoning — vinegars, citrus, wine, and 발효 sources like 김치 brine cut richness, brighten flat dishes, and give the palate contrast it craves. Learning when to add acid (and which one) turns "something's missing" into a finished dish, and makes acids interchangeable by matching their pH and flavor character.

## Behavior / Mechanism
- [fact] Cooking vinegars run pH ~2-3 (≥4% acetic acid by US law); citrus juice ~2-2.5; wine ~3-4. Lower pH = sharper, more sour perception #ph
- [technique] Add acid late or off-heat for brightness — its volatile aromatics and sharp top notes fade with prolonged cooking, leaving only flat sourness #timing
- [technique] Add acid early (braises, marinades) when you want it to tenderize, balance, or mellow into the background — long cooking rounds harshness #timing
- [fact] Acid denatures proteins (ceviche "cooks" fish; lemon firms egg in sauces) and breaks down some pectin/cell walls, but can toughen beans if added before they soften #protein
- [fact] Acid keeps green vegetables and cut fruit from browning short-term but dulls bright-green chlorophyll over long cooking — so add to greens at the very end #color
- [fact] Acid balances salt, fat, and sweetness on the palate; a squeeze of lemon makes a dish taste "seasoned" without more salt #balance
- [fact] Vinegars are themselves products of fermentation (acetic acid bacteria turning alcohol to acid) — flavor varies hugely: rice (mild, sweet), 발사믹 (sweet-complex), red wine (sharp), 현미식초/사과식초 (fruity) #fermentation

## Selection
- [fact] Match the acid to the cuisine and richness: rice vinegar for Asian dressings, lemon/lime for bright fresh dishes, red wine vinegar for hearty sauces, balsamic for sweet-savory glazes #selection
- [fact] Fresh-squeezed citrus >> bottled; zest carries aromatic oils that juice lacks, for fragrance without extra sourness #citrus
- [fact] Wine for cooking should be drinkable, not labeled "cooking wine" (salted, off-flavored); its acid + alcohol deglaze and add depth #wine

## Creative Application & Substitution
Use acid as a finishing brightener the way you use salt: when a soup, stew, or sauce tastes dull or heavy, add a teaspoon of vinegar or a squeeze of citrus before reaching for more salt. Substitute by pH and flavor — lemon ↔ rice vinegar ↔ white wine vinegar work in many dressings; use 김치 brine or pickle juice as a tangy, umami-rich acid in dressings and braises. Layer two acids (e.g. lime for sharpness + a splash of vinegar for depth) the way a chef layers aromatics.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- [fact] Flat, heavy dish that more salt won't fix → it usually needs acid #fix
- [fact] Too sour: balance with fat (butter/cream), a touch of sweetness, or salt — don't try to cook it off #fix
- [technique] Acid curdles dairy — add to cream sauces off-heat or use high-fat cream (see [[Dairy]]) #fix
- [fact] Beans/legumes stay hard if acid added early — soften first, acidify after #fix
