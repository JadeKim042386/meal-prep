# Herbs
Herbs are the leafy aromatic edge of a dish — their flavor lives in fragile volatile oils that brighten, freshen, and signal a cuisine before you even taste. The single most useful mental model is **soft vs hardy**, because it predicts everything: when to add the herb, whether it survives drying, and how much heat it tolerates. Treating herbs as a finishing instrument (not a background note) is one of the cheapest ways to make food taste alive.

## Behavior / Role
- [fact] Soft herbs (basil, cilantro, parsley, mint, dill, chives, tarragon) have tender leaves and delicate, water-soluble/volatile aromatics that evaporate fast under heat — add at the END or off-heat #soft-herbs
- [fact] Hardy herbs (rosemary, thyme, oregano, sage, bay, marjoram) have woody stems, leathery leaves, and robust oils that withstand long cooking — add EARLY so their oils infuse the fat/liquid #hardy-herbs
- [technique] Basil added in the last ~2 minutes retains 90%+ of its aroma; added at the start of a simmer it drops toward ~35% as volatiles boil off #volatility
- [fact] Fresh herbs added too early can turn dull or faintly bitter once their bright top-notes flash off, leaving behind heavier compounds #volatility
- [pairing] Basil + tomato is canonical — basil restores the green/grassy notes tomato loses to heat or canning; cilantro + tomato is the salsa axis; mint + lamb uses mint's cleansing menthol to deodorize lamb's funk #pairing
- [pairing] Rosemary, thyme, sage, oregano, bay all pair with tomato-based braises and roasts; lamb has broad affinity for the whole herbal family #pairing
- [fact] Drying concentrates flavor by removing water, so dried herbs are stronger by volume but have lost the fresh top-notes #dried

## Selection / Handling
- [fact] Choose bunches with perky, unwilted leaves and no blackening or slime; smell them — vivid aroma at the store predicts vivid aroma on the plate #selection
- [technique] Store soft herbs like cut flowers (stems in water, loose bag in fridge); wrap hardy herbs in a barely damp towel #storage
- [fact] Fresh-to-dried conversion is roughly 3:1 (1 Tbsp fresh ≈ 1 tsp dried) because dried is concentrated #ratio
- [fact] Hardy herbs (thyme, oregano, bay, rosemary) dry well and retain a large share of their oils; soft herbs (basil, cilantro, parsley) lose 50–60% of their delicate aromatics and dried versions taste like hay #dried
- [technique] Bloom dried hardy herbs in warm fat at the start to re-solubilize their oils, the same logic as blooming ground spices #technique

## Creative Application & Substitution
Think in two moves: an **infusion herb** (hardy, added early to flavor the cooking medium) and a **finishing herb** (soft, added raw at the end for lift). A braise can carry both — a thyme/bay sachet during the cook, a fistful of parsley/dill stirred in at service. To substitute, swap within the same class and aroma family: parsley↔chervil↔cilantro for green lift; rosemary↔thyme↔oregano↔sage for woody/savory depth. Cross-class swaps fail (dumping rosemary where you wanted basil will dominate). Make a quick herb oil or salsa verde to deploy soft-herb brightness onto roasted, grilled, or braised food that has gone flat. Stems of soft herbs (cilantro, parsley) carry more flavor than the leaves — mince them in rather than discarding.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Dish tastes flat after long cooking → you used soft herbs too early; finish with a fresh handful off-heat.
- Dried herb tastes dusty/bitter → it was old (replace yearly) or a soft herb that doesn't dry well; switch to fresh.
- Rosemary/sage overpowering → these are high-impact; use a light hand and remove woody sprigs before serving.
- Herb oil turned bitter → don't over-blend warm; blanch-shock soft herbs and blend cold for vivid green oil.
