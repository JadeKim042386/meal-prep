# Seasonality and Selection
Peak-season produce tastes better, costs less, and needs almost nothing done to it — which is exactly why seasonality is a creativity engine, not a constraint. When an ingredient is at its flavor maximum, you cook *minimally and around it* rather than masking it; when the market shifts, your menu shifts with it, forcing new pairings you'd never plan in the abstract. Learning to *select* at peak (using every sense) is the other half: the best technique can't rescue a flavorless, out-of-season tomato.

## Behavior / Role
- [fact] In-season produce is harvested ripe near its peak, so flavor compounds and sugars are highest and transport/storage degradation is lowest — better taste, lower price #peak-season
- [fact] Out-of-season produce is often picked unripe for shipping and gas-ripened; texture may recover but aroma volatiles and sweetness don't fully develop #out-of-season
- [fact] Ripeness raises glutamate and sugar in fruits like tomato — a vine-ripe summer tomato carries far more savory-sweet depth than a winter one, which is why season dictates whether you eat it raw or must cook/concentrate it #ripeness
- [technique] Selection is multisensory: weight (heavy = juicy), aroma (fragrant at the stem = ripe), give (slight yield on stone fruit/avocado), color and taut skin, perky leaves/stems #selection
- [fact] Some fruit ripens after picking (climacteric: peach, banana, avocado, tomato), some does NOT (strawberry, grape, citrus, cherry) — buy non-climacteric fruit already ripe #climacteric
- [pairing] "What grows together goes together": peak-season ingredients of the same moment tend to pair (tomato+basil+summer; squash+sage+autumn) — season is itself a pairing guide #season-pairing

## Selection / Handling
- [fact] Strawberries don't ripen post-harvest — pick firm, plump, uniformly deep-red berries with no white tips or green shoulders #strawberry
- [fact] Stone fruit (peach, plum, nectarine): gentle give near the stem and a sweet fragrance signal ripeness #stone-fruit
- [fact] Avocado/kiwi: a gentle squeeze with slight give means ready; rock-hard needs days, mushy is overripe #squeeze-test
- [technique] Shop the market and let availability lead — build the dish around what looks best that day rather than forcing a fixed shopping list #market-led
- [fact] Buy peak-season gluts to preserve (freeze, dry, ferment, can) so a great-season flavor carries into the off-season #preserve

## Creative Application & Substitution
Flip the workflow: instead of choosing a recipe then sourcing ingredients, choose the best ingredient at the market then build a dish around it — peak asparagus becomes the star with just salt, fat, acid, and heat. Use seasonal swaps as forced creativity: a "tomato + basil" template in spring becomes "peas + mint," in autumn "roasted squash + sage" — same salad/pasta structure, new season (this is role-based substitution applied to time). When a hero ingredient is out of season, don't fake it — substitute the *role* it played (sweet-acidic brightness, fresh crunch) with whatever IS at peak, and you'll often discover a better dish. Off-season, switch techniques: concentrate weak winter tomatoes by roasting or use canned/sun-dried (higher glutamate) rather than serving them raw.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Watery, bland tomato/strawberry → out of season; cook/concentrate it, or swap to a peak-season fruit's role.
- Hard, never-ripening peach/avocado bought "ripe" → it was picked too green; buy climacteric fruit a few days ahead and let it ripen at room temp.
- Mealy, off-texture produce → over-refrigerated (tomatoes, stone fruit lose flavor cold); store at room temp until ripe.
- Menu feels stale → you're ignoring the season; let the market dictate a swap and the dish renews itself.
