# Fermented Condiments
Fermented condiments are the salt-and-umami workhorses of Asian cooking: fermentation breaks soy or fish protein into free amino acids (chiefly glutamate), so a single spoonful delivers saltiness, deep umami, and aromatic funk at once. They are *structural* ingredients, not table garnishes — in Korean cooking 간장 and 된장 are built into a dish at every stage. Knowing each one's salt-to-umami-to-funk balance lets you season with character instead of plain salt.

## Behavior / Role
- [fact] These pastes/sauces do three jobs simultaneously — salt, umami (free glutamate from broken-down protein), and fermented aroma — which is why they read as "deeper" than salt alone #workhorse
- [fact] 간장 (Korean soy / ganjang) and soy sauce ~782 mg glutamate/100g; salty + savory, a liquid seasoning. 국간장 (soup soy) is saltier/lighter for seasoning broths without darkening them #glutamate
- [fact] 된장 (doenjang): soybeans + salt (traditionally no grain), earthy, pungent, deeply savory — more robust and funkier than miso; the base of 된장찌개 #doenjang
- [fact] Miso: soybeans + salt + 코지 (Aspergillus oryzae kōji), often with rice or barley — milder, often slightly sweet; white/shiro is gentle, red/aka is bold #miso
- [fact] 고추장: fermented chile paste with glutinous rice + 메주 powder + 고춧가루 — uniquely sweet-spicy-savory-funky, no true counterpart in other cuisines #gochujang
- [fact] Fish sauce (느억맘/남쁠라): salted fermented fish, intense glutamate + free amino acids; tiny amounts add backbone umami and salt to SE Asian food #fish-sauce
- [fact] 두반장 (doubanjiang): fermented broad-bean + chile paste, the salty-spicy-umami soul of Sichuan cooking (mapo tofu) #doubanjiang
- [pairing] Glutamate-rich condiments synergize with inosinate sources (meat, 가다랑어포, anchovy) for an ~8× umami boost — e.g. doenjang stew with beef/anchovy stock #synergy

## Selection / Handling
- [fact] Traditional 된장 and 간장 are co-products: 메주 (fermented soybean blocks) brined; the liquid becomes 간장, the solids become 된장 #provenance
- [fact] Read labels — "naturally brewed/양조" soy beats fast acid-hydrolyzed; longer-aged jang and miso are deeper and more expensive #selection
- [technique] Don't boil delicate miso hard — stir it in near the end to keep its aroma and live character; 된장 is sturdier and is simmered into stews #handling
- [fact] These are high in sodium — they ARE the salt; cut or omit added salt when using them generously #salt
- [fact] Refrigerate after opening; the high salt keeps them stable for months, flavor slowly deepens/darkens #storage

## Creative Application & Substitution
Use them as a flavor "anchor": whisk miso or doenjang into a dressing, marinade, glaze, or compound butter to give a Western dish a savory backbone (miso butter on corn, doenjang in a vinaigrette). Caramelize a spoonful of gochujang or doubanjiang in oil at the start of a braise to bloom its chile and deepen color. To substitute by role (salt + umami): soy sauce ↔ fish sauce ↔ a little Worcestershire (all salty-umami liquids); doenjang ↔ red miso + a pinch of salt (doenjang is funkier, so go light); gochujang ↔ miso + gochugaru + a touch of honey. Always re-balance salt and sweetness after swapping, since each carries a different ratio.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Dish too salty → you salted on top of an already-salty paste; dilute, add fat/acid, or bulk up the base.
- Miso soup tastes flat/cooked-out → you boiled the miso; add it off the boil at the end.
- Raw, harsh chile-paste edge → bloom gochujang/doubanjiang in oil to round it before adding liquid.
- Want depth without saltiness → reach for low-sodium umami boosters (kombu, mushroom) instead of more jang.
