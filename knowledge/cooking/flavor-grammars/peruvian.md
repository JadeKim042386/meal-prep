# Peruvian Flavor Grammar
Peru is constitutionally a fusion cuisine: the native Andean/coastal larder (potato, corn, ají, fish) layered with Spanish, then decisively with Chinese (Chifa) and Japanese (Nikkei) waves. Its flavor logic rests on three pillars — a fried aromatic base (aderezo), fruity-to-fiery ají chiles, and a citrus-forward raw-fish tradition (ceviche) supercharged by Asian technique. Lime is the central acid; umami runs unusually deep (ají pastes, soy/sillao, dried fish, MSG).

## Flavor Base / Aromatics
- [fact] Aderezo = Peru's sofrito: red onion + garlic sweated slowly in oil until tender (not brown), then ají paste fried until it "curdles," then cumin bloomed in; base for rice, seco, soups, stews #aderezo
- [fact] Ají amarillo is the soul of Peruvian food (Acurio: "the most important ingredient"; Martínez: "the DNA") — fruity, orange-yellow, manageable heat #aji-amarillo
- [pairing] The ají trio: ají amarillo (fruity, mild-medium), ají panca (mild dark-red, smoky-sweet, the marinade chile for anticuchos), rocoto (hot red highland chile) #aji-trio
- [pairing] Huacatay (Peruvian black mint — mint+basil+tarragon+citrus, bitter-earthy finish), culantro/cilantro; garlic + red onion backbone #huacatay-herbs

## Acid / Fat / Heat / Umami
- [pairing] Acid: lime (limón, the small key-lime type) is central and non-negotiable — it seasons AND "cooks" the fish; vinegar deglazes the wok in lomo saltado #acid
- [pairing] Fat: neutral vegetable oil dominates (aderezo, frying); lard in some traditional dishes; olive oil + botija olives in causa/huancaína #fat
- [fact] Heat graded by ají: amarillo mild-fruity, ají limo (coastal, into ceviches), rocoto serious heat (~50k-250k SHU, boiled before use to tame) #heat
- [pairing] Umami unusually deep: ají pastes, garlic, huacatay, soy sauce (sillao, via Chifa), dried fish/fish trimmings (in leche de tigre), and aji-no-moto/MSG (genuinely ubiquitous in Peru) #umami

## Signature Combinations
- [pairing] Ají amarillo + garlic + cumin (in oil) = the aderezo, the savory backbone of nearly every cooked dish #aderezo-chord
- [pairing] Lime + fresh fish + ají + red onion + cilantro = ceviche / leche de tigre #ceviche
- [pairing] Soy (sillao) + lime + ají amarillo over thin-sliced raw fish = tiradito (Nikkei) #tiradito
- [pairing] Soy + vinegar + wok char + beef + red onion + tomato = lomo saltado (Chifa) #lomo-saltado
- [pairing] Ají amarillo + queso fresco + evaporated milk + saltine crackers = papa a la huancaína sauce #huancaina

## Creative Application
Ceviche: very fresh fish "cooked" (protein-denatured) by lime acid; leche de tigre ("tiger's milk") = lime + fish trimmings + onion + garlic + ají + cilantro, blended and strained — the Nikkei revolution shortened marination to near-instant, so fish must be sashimi-grade. Served with sweet camote, choclo (Andean corn), and cancha (toasted corn nuts) for sweetness/crunch against the acid. THE key insight: in Peru, "authentic" already includes soy, ginger, the wok, sashimi cuts, and MSG — fusion IS the grammar. The reusable axes: fruity-ají base + bright lime acid + an umami amplifier (sillao/MSG/fish) + a sweet-starchy-crunchy garnish triad. Riff A — "Tiradito de hongos": roasted king-oyster "sashimi" dressed with a kombu-dashi leche de tigre (soy + lime + ají amarillo + huacatay), cancha for crunch. Riff B — "Chaufa-style roasted squash": wok-char squash + red onion, deglaze with sillao + vinegar, ají panca for smoky depth, finish with a fried egg over rice.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Over-curing ceviche → rubbery, dry fish: use sashimi-grade fish, marinate only a couple of minutes, dress just before serving.
- Harsh one-note lime acid: build proper leche de tigre with fish trimmings/stock to round the acid and add umami; balance with the sweet camote.
- Aderezo browned or rushed: onions must sweat soft and "undetectable," ají fried until it curdles, cumin bloomed — skip this and dishes taste raw/flat.
- Rocoto too aggressive: boil (sometimes twice, changing water) before stuffing/blending to tame the heat.
- Watery causa / thin huancaína: use dry starchy yellow potato for causa; thicken huancaína with crushed saltines so the sauce coats.
