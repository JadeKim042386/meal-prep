# Chinese Flavor Grammar

Chinese cooking is not one grammar but a family of regional dialects sharing a core aromatic trinity — 생강 (ginger) + 파 (scallion) + 마늘 (garlic) — plus soy, vinegar, and rice wine. Each region then bends the rules: Cantonese chases freshness and 镬气 (wok hei), Sichuan composes layered 复合味 (compound flavors) around 麻辣 (mala), Hunan goes aggressively chili-sour-smoky. Heat control (high for stir-fry, slow for braises) is itself a flavor tool.

## Flavor Base / Aromatics
- [pairing] The pan-Chinese aromatic trinity is 姜葱蒜 — 생강 (ginger) + 파 (scallion) + 마늘 (garlic), hit in hot oil to open a dish #aromatics
- [fact] Core seasonings: 간장 (light + dark soy), 식초 (Chinkiang black + rice vinegar), 绍兴酒 (Shaoxing wine), sugar, white pepper #pantry
- [pairing] Cantonese (광둥) prizes 鲜 (xiān, fresh-sweet) — light soy, sugar, ginger-scallion, oyster sauce; minimal masking, 镬气 (wok hei) the smoky breath of the wok #cantonese
- [pairing] Sichuan (쓰촨) builds on 豆瓣酱 (doubanjiang, fermented chili-bean paste), 花椒 (Sichuan peppercorn, numbing 麻), dried chilies, 豆豉 (fermented black beans) #sichuan
- [pairing] Hunan (후난) leans fresh/pickled chili, garlic, and smoke-cured (腊) meats — hotter and more sour-savory than Sichuan, less numbing #hunan

## Acid / Fat / Heat / Umami
- [pairing] Acid: 镇江香醋 (Chinkiang black vinegar, malty) and rice vinegar; central to 酸辣 (hot-sour) and 鱼香 (fish-fragrant) #acid
- [pairing] Fat: rendered lard, peanut/neutral oil at high heat; 红油 (chili oil) as a flavored fat; 참기름 as finish #fat
- [pairing] Heat: dried chilies + 豆瓣酱 (Sichuan) vs fresh/pickled chili (Hunan); 花椒 adds numbing 麻 distinct from chili 辣 #heat
- [pairing] Umami: soy, oyster sauce, 豆豉, fermented bean pastes, dried shrimp/scallop, 라오간마 — fermentation + drying stacked #umami
- [fact] 镬气 (wok hei) — high-heat Maillard/caramelization aroma from a screaming carbon-steel wok — is a flavor created by technique, not an ingredient #wokhei

## Signature Combinations
- [pairing] 麻辣 (mala): 花椒 (numbing) + dried chili / 豆瓣酱 (hot) — the Sichuan signature (麻婆豆腐, hotpot) #mala
- [pairing] 鱼香 (yuxiang, fish-fragrant): pickled chili + 마늘 + 생강 + 파 + sugar + vinegar + soy — sweet-sour-savory-spicy with NO fish #yuxiang
- [pairing] 姜葱 (ginger-scallion): 생강 + 파 + oil + light soy — the clean Cantonese signature for steamed fish/chicken #gingerscallion
- [pairing] 酸辣 (hot-sour): black vinegar + white pepper + chili — the hot-and-sour soup axis #hotsour
- [pairing] 怪味 (guaiwei, strange flavor): sweet + sour + salty + numbing + spicy + sesame in balance, none dominant #guaiwei

## Creative Application
Decide which regional dialect you're speaking first, because it sets the rules. Cantonese: minimal seasoning, ginger-scallion-light soy, fast high heat to chase wok hei, let the ingredient shine. Sichuan: bloom doubanjiang in oil until red and fragrant, layer chili + 花椒, then balance with a touch of sugar and vinegar to make a compound flavor. Example riffs: a yuxiang-sauced roasted eggplant (the sauce, not the fish, carries the idiom); a mala-spiced chickpea stir-fry blooming 花椒 and doubanjiang in oil. Always start aromatics in hot oil; never crowd the pan if you want wok hei.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- No wok hei because the pan is too cool/crowded: heat hard, cook in batches, keep food moving.
- Treating Sichuan as just "spicy": without 花椒 (numbing) and the sweet-sour balance, it's flat heat, not mala.
- Skipping the bloom of doubanjiang/chili in oil: raw bean paste tastes harsh and salty; frying it releases color and aroma.
- Using only light soy: dark soy adds color and caramel depth to braises — they are not interchangeable.
- Forgetting Shaoxing wine and a pinch of sugar: they round and balance saltiness in many savory sauces.
