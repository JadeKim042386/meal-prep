# Umami and Synergy

Umami (감칠맛) is the savory, mouth-filling, lingering taste of glutamate — and its superpower is synergy: combine free glutamate with a 5'-ribonucleotide (inosinate or guanylate) and perceived savoriness multiplies far beyond the sum of the parts. This single mechanism explains 다시마+가다랑어포 dashi, 토마토+파르미지아노, and almost every deeply savory dish across cultures. Mastering which foods carry which molecule lets you engineer depth on demand.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [fact] Umami is the taste of free glutamate (an amino acid), sensed by T1R1/T1R3 receptors; sources also include aspartate #umami
- [fact] Synergy: glutamate (MSG/glutamate) + inosinate (IMP) or guanylate (GMP) produces a peak ~7–8x the umami intensity of either alone, maximal near a 1:1 glutamate:inosinate ratio #synergy
- [fact] The nucleotides have little umami by themselves — they act mainly as amplifiers of glutamate by stabilizing the receptor's active state #synergy
- [pairing] Glutamate-rich + nucleotide-rich = synergy. Pair a glutamate food with an inosinate or guanylate food. #pairing
- [fact] Glutamate carriers: 다시마(kombu), 토마토 (esp. concentrated/sun-dried), 파르미지아노/aged cheese, 간장·된장 (soy/miso), 멸치, peas, corn, 김 #umami
- [fact] Inosinate (IMP) carriers: 가다랑어포(katsuobushi/bonito), 멸치, dried fish, cured/aged meat, pork, chicken, beef #synergy
- [fact] Guanylate (GMP) carriers: 표고(dried shiitake) — by far the strongest; other dried mushrooms #synergy
- [fact] Drying/aging multiplies nucleotides: drying bonito can raise inosinate ~30-fold as post-mortem ATP breaks down #fact

## Why It Works
Glutamate binds the T1R1/T1R3 receptor; the 5'-ribonucleotides bind a separate site on the same receptor and lock it in a conformation that grips glutamate much more tightly. The result is allosteric potentiation, not addition — small amounts of an IMP/GMP source dramatically boost a glutamate source. Sensory panels using glutamate-inosinate mixtures find the synergy peaks at roughly a 1:1 ratio, where intensity reaches about 7–8x that of either component alone; some industry figures cite even higher (up to ~15x or more) depending on concentration and the nucleotide used. Traditional cooks discovered this empirically: analysis of dashi from a top Japanese restaurant found the glutamate-from-다시마 and inosinate-from-가다랑어포 sitting near that optimal 1:1 proportion. The same logic underlies 토마토 sauce + Parmesan, beef + cheese in French onion soup, and meat + tomato in bolognese.

## Creative Application
To deepen any dish, draw one ingredient from the glutamate column and one from the nucleotide column. Vegetarian depth without meat: 다시마 (glutamate) + 표고 (guanylate) = a fully synergistic plant stock — this is shojin/temple dashi. Build a "umami trifecta" for a braise: 토마토 paste + 간장/된장 (glutamate) + dried 표고 or anchovy (nucleotide) + aged Parmesan rind. Reverse-engineer existing classics: caesar dressing (anchovy IMP + Parmesan glutamate), ramen (kombu/glutamate + pork/chicken bones IMP). For a quick fix, a pinch of MSG plus a little dried-mushroom or anchovy powder gives instant synergy.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Stacking two glutamate sources but no nucleotide → savory but flat; you miss the synergy multiplier. Always cross the columns.
- Boiling 다시마 hard → bitterness and sliminess (alginates); steep gently, remove before a hard boil.
- Umami amplifies salt perception, so over-salting umami-rich dishes is easy — taste, then salt last.
- Too much umami + salt with no acid → heavy, cloying; brighten with 식초/citrus (see acidity).
