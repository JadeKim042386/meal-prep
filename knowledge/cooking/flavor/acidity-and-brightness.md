# Acidity and Brightness

Acid is the "brightness" knob — the sour tang that wakes up a dish, cuts richness, and makes every other flavor pop. It is often the missing element when a well-salted, savory dish still tastes heavy or dull. Because different acids carry different aromas and intensities, choosing *which* acid is itself a creative decision.

## Principle / Mechanism
- [principle] Sourness is the tongue's response to H+ ions (low pH); perceived intensity depends on the acid type, not just pH #acid
- [pairing] Acid balances fat and richness (fat ↔ acid is a core axis) and lifts flat dishes by contrast #pairing
- [fact] Acid suppresses perceived sweetness and bitterness, and sharpens the impression of other flavors — a brightening, focusing effect #cross-modal
- [fact] Acetic acid (식초/vinegar) — sharp, pungent, volatile aroma; assertive, fades less on cooking #acid
- [fact] Citric acid (lemon/lime, 레몬·라임) — clean, bright, fruity; aroma from the zest's terpenes, not the acid itself #acid
- [fact] Lactic acid (yogurt, 김치, 사워크라우트, fermented) — mild, round, "tangy-creamy," low volatility #acid
- [fact] Malic acid (green apple, grape, stone fruit) — crisp, lingering, slightly harsh #acid
- [fact] Tartaric (grape/wine) and tannins add structure; acid in wine carries both sourness and astringency #acid
- [principle] Dishes generally want acid added near the END — heat dulls and drives off aroma-bearing acids #acid

## Why It Works
The tongue's sour receptors fire on protons (H+). But equal pH does not mean equal sourness: weak acids like acetic and citric release H+ gradually and also carry their own aromas, so they "taste" different despite similar acidity. Acid's culinary power is largely about contrast and suppression. By contrast, a sour note cuts through fat and umami heaviness — the saliva-stimulating tang resets the palate, which is why a squeeze of lemon makes a buttery fish or a rich braise suddenly taste cleaner and more vivid. Chemically, acid also suppresses the perception of sweetness and bitterness, so adding acid to an over-sweet or over-bitter dish rebalances it without removing anything. Because most of citrus's appeal is the volatile terpenes in the zest/oil (limonene), lemon and lime brighten partly through aroma, not just acidity — which is why bottled lemon juice tastes flat compared with a fresh squeeze.

## Creative Application
Treat acid as a finishing instrument matched to the dish's aroma. Rich/fatty dish that tastes heavy → a few drops of vinegar or lemon at the end, not more salt. Match the acid to the cuisine and aroma: 식초 for stir-fries and pickles, lemon/lime for bright herb-forward dishes, lactic (yogurt, 김치국물) for creamy or fermented depth, verjus/wine for sauces. Build "layers" of acid: a long-cooked acid for body (tomato, wine reduced into a braise) plus a raw acid at the finish for sparkle. Example: a stew can taste muddy until a splash of red-wine vinegar at service makes the meat and umami snap into focus.

## Pitfalls / Fixes
- Too sour → add fat (cream, butter, oil), a pinch of sugar, or starch (potato, rice) to round it; a little salt also blunts harsh acidity.
- Adding all the acid early → it cooks off and tastes flat; reserve a final hit for service.
- Using acid to fix a dish that actually lacks salt or umami → diagnose first (see Taste and Aroma Perception).
- Bottled citrus juice → loses zest aromatics; finish with fresh juice or zest.
